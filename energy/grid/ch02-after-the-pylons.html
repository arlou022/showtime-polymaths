    What Happens After the Pylons?

    From the grid supply point to your street: the voltage ladder, the green box at the end of the road, and why a sunny afternoon can be a problem.

    So what happens after the pylons?

    Last chapter we followed electricity from the power station up to 400,000 volts and onto the pylons. And we know it arrives at your socket at 230 volts. But what happens in between?

    Somewhere near you, maybe at the end of your road, there’s a green metal box behind a fence with a yellow “Danger of death” sign. Or a grey drum bolted to a wooden pole. That’s the last stop before your house. Between it and the pylons sits a whole hidden network of cables, substations and transformers. It’s called the distribution network.

    It’s easy to think of it as the boring bit, just the last few miles. But today it’s where most of the action is. Solar panels, batteries, electric cars, heat pumps and data centres all connect here. And it was never designed for any of them.

    Contents

      The handover: the grid supply point

      The pylons don’t run all the way to your town. At a big substation called a grid supply point (GSP), transformers step the voltage down from 400,000 or 275,000 volts to 132,000. This is the handover. Above it, the network belongs to the transmission company. Below it, it belongs to the local distribution network operator (DNO).

      Careful: Scotland draws the line differently

      In England and Wales, 132 kV counts as distribution. In Scotland it counts as transmission, so the handover happens lower down. It’s a historical quirk of how the networks were set up, not a difference in the physics.

      The voltage ladder

      From the grid supply point, the voltage comes down in stages, like steps on a ladder:

      StepVoltageWhat it does
      Grid supply point400 or 275 kV → 132 kVHands power from transmission to the DNO
      Bulk supply point132 kV → 33 kVFeeds a city or a large rural area
      Primary substation33 kV → 11 kVFeeds a town or a few neighbourhoods
      Secondary substation11 kV → 400 VThe green box or pole drum feeding your street
      Your house230 VOne phase of the 400 V supply
      Where does this come from? Why 11, 33 and 132?

      Notice they’re all multiples of 11. The usual explanation goes back to the early 1900s. Engineers designed equipment for round numbers like 10, 30 and 120 kV. But voltage drops along a line (\(V = IR\)), so they sent power out about 10% higher, to make sure it still arrived at roughly the round number.

      1.1 × 10 = 11, 1.1 × 30 = 33 and 1.1 × 120 = 132. The numbers stuck and became standards.

      Big factories and solar farms connect higher up the ladder, at 11, 33 or 132 kV. Homes connect at the bottom.

      And why does the street get 400 V but your house 230 V? Your street gets three-phase power, the same three wires a third of a cycle apart that we met in Chapter 1. The voltage between two of the phase wires is 400 V. Your house connects to just one phase and the neutral wire, which is smaller by a factor of √3:

      V_{\text{house}} = \dfrac{V_{\text{phase-to-phase}}}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{400}{1.73} \approx 230\ \text{V}
      Where does this come from? Why √3?

      The three phases peak a third of a cycle apart. When you measure between two of them, you get the difference of two waves that are out of step, and that difference works out √3 ≈ 1.73 times bigger than either wave on its own.

      It’s the same √3 that appeared in the generator current in Chapter 1. Houses along a street are shared out between the three phases, so the load stays roughly balanced.

      Why not jump straight from 132,000 V to 230 V?

      It’s the same trade-off as last chapter, just in reverse. High voltage is cheap for moving lots of power a long way. But high-voltage equipment is big and expensive. It needs thick insulation, big gaps and large, fenced-off substations. You can’t run 132,000 volts down every residential street.

      So each step down covers a shorter distance and carries less power, using smaller, cheaper kit. By the time you reach your street, it’s a cable under the pavement feeding a hundred or so homes.

      Analogy: roads

      Transmission is the motorway. 132 kV and 33 kV are the A roads between towns. 11 kV is the B roads through neighbourhoods. 400 V is your street. You wouldn’t build a motorway to every front door, and you wouldn’t drive from Cardiff to London on side streets.

      This also explains a number from last chapter. Transmission loses about 2%, but the local networks lose roughly another 5%.

      Where does this come from? Why do the local networks lose more?

      The voltage is lower, so for the same power the current is higher, and the loss grows with current squared (\(I^2R\)).

      The last few miles are the most wasteful part of the journey.

      Who runs it: the DNOs

      Basically, after the industry was nationalised in 1948, local electricity was run by regional boards. When it was privatised around 1990, those regions became the 14 distribution areas still used today. Over time, takeovers left them run by six companies:

      • National Grid Electricity Distribution (NGED): South Wales, the South West, and the East and West Midlands. It was called Western Power Distribution until National Grid bought it in 2021 and renamed it in 2022.
      • UK Power Networks: London, the South East and the East of England.
      • SSEN Distribution: the north of Scotland and central southern England.
      • SP Energy Networks: central and southern Scotland, Merseyside and North Wales.
      • Northern Powergrid: the North East and Yorkshire.
      • Electricity North West: the North West. Since 2024 it has had the same owner as SP Energy Networks: Iberdrola, the Spanish company that owns ScottishPower.

      Each one is a natural monopoly. It would make no sense to have two sets of cables down every street, so you can’t choose your DNO. Instead, the regulator Ofgem sets how much each one can spend and earn, in five-year price controls. The next one, ED3, runs from 2028 to 2033.

      Careful: your DNO isn’t your supplier

      The company on your bill, like Octopus or British Gas, is your supplier. It buys electricity and sells it to you. The DNO owns the cables that deliver it, and gets paid through a slice of your bill. If there’s a power cut, you call the DNO (or 105), not your supplier.

      Built for a one-way world

      For most of the last century, distribution was simple. Power flowed one way, downhill: from big power stations, through the ladder, to homes and businesses.

      So DNOs designed the network for the worst case, usually a cold winter evening, and then mostly left it alone. Engineers call this “fit and forget”. If the cables were big enough for the peak, nobody needed to watch them closely.

      Then the world changed. Solar farms, wind turbines and batteries started connecting at 11 kV, 33 kV and 132 kV. Millions of homes put solar panels on their roofs. This is called embedded generation: generation inside the distribution network, rather than at a big power station.

      On a sunny afternoon, a village with a solar farm can produce more power than it uses. The extra has nowhere to go but back up the ladder. This is reverse power flow, and parts of the network were never designed for it.

      The two ways a network gets full

      There are two main limits on how much a network can carry.

      1. Heat

      This is the same as the pylons in Chapter 1. Current heats cables and transformers (\(I^2R\)). Too much for too long, and the insulation degrades or the transformer overheats. Every cable and transformer has a thermal rating. It doesn’t matter which way the power is flowing. Too much current is too much current.

      2. Voltage

      This is the less obvious one. Remember Ohm’s law: when current flows along a cable, some voltage is lost along the way.

      \Delta V = I R

      So the houses at the end of a long street get a slightly lower voltage than the ones near the substation. DNOs plan for this. They set the transformer slightly high, so that even the last house gets enough voltage on a busy winter evening.

      But when power flows back, the effect reverses. The voltage at the far end rises above the transformer’s. And the law says your supply has to stay between 216 V and 253 V, the same tolerance from Chapter 1. Above 253 V the DNO is outside its legal limit. Push higher still, to about 262 V, and solar inverters are designed to switch themselves off within about a second, to protect your appliances.

      Where does this come from? Why do inverters wait until 262 V?

      UK rules for small generators (an engineering standard called G98, and G99 for bigger ones) set the inverter’s first over-voltage trip at 262.2 V, held for about a second.

      That’s 230 V plus 14%, a bit above the network’s +10% legal limit, so inverters don’t switch off over small, brief overshoots.

      Worked example: a sunny day on a long street

      A street cable has a resistance of 0.1 Ω from the substation to the last house. The transformer is set to 245 V.

      Winter evening: homes draw 150 A from the substation. Voltage drop = 150 × 0.1 = 15 V. The last house gets 245 − 15 = 230 V. ✓

      Sunny summer afternoon: rooftop solar pushes 150 A back towards the substation. Now the voltage rises by 15 V. The last house sees 245 + 15 = 260 V, above the 253 V legal limit. ✗ A little more sunshine and it passes about 262 V, where the inverters at the end of the street start switching off.

      Setting the transformer high for winter makes summer worse. That’s why many rural streets struggle with solar long before any cable overheats.

      Careful: this is simplified

      Real cables also have reactance (an AC effect), three phases, and many houses spread along the cable rather than one at the end. Some newer transformers can adjust their voltage automatically through the day. But the core idea is exactly this: current flowing back raises the voltage.

      Headroom, and why not everyone boils the kettle at once

      How much more can a substation take before it hits one of those limits? That spare room is called headroom. It comes in two kinds:

      • Demand headroom: room for more load, like EV chargers, heat pumps or a data centre.
      • Generation headroom: room for more generation, like a solar farm or a battery exporting.

      A substation can have plenty of one and none of the other. DNOs such as NGED publish maps of both, so developers can see where they might connect.

      Now here’s a neat question. A kettle draws 3 kW, a shower can draw 9 kW, and an oven another 2 or 3. So why can a transformer built for about 2 kW per house cope at all?

      The answer is diversity. Not everyone boils the kettle at the same moment. Across a hundred homes, the peaks are spread out. So the average peak per house is much lower than any single house’s peak.

      Where does this come from? How low is the peak per house?

      DNOs measure it. Across a large group of homes, the peak demand per house traditionally works out at roughly 1 to 2 kW, even though each house can briefly draw ten times that.

      Engineers call it after-diversity maximum demand. The more homes you add up, the more the individual spikes smooth out, so the lower the figure gets.

      Electric cars and heat pumps break this. They don’t draw power for two minutes like a kettle. They run for hours, and often at the same times as everyone else’s: cold evenings for heat pumps, and straight after work for cars.

      Worked example: an EV on every other drive

      A street transformer can supply 300 kW. Its 100 homes peak at about 2 kW each after diversity, so 200 kW. Comfortably fine.

      Now 40 of those homes get a 7 kW car charger, and half of them plug in at 6 pm. That’s 20 × 7 = 140 kW extra, all at once. 200 + 140 = 340 kW. The transformer is now overloaded, every weekday evening.

      Spread the charging over the night, and the same cars fit easily. Same energy, different timing. That’s the whole idea behind smart charging.

      Careful: kW and kVA

      Engineers rate transformers in kVA rather than kW, because AC equipment has to carry some current that does no useful work. For this chapter, treating them as the same is close enough.

      The blind spot

      Here’s something surprising. DNOs know a lot about the top of the ladder. Grid supply points and primary substations have sensors and send live readings back to a control room.

      But at the bottom, most of the hundreds of thousands of street transformers have no monitoring at all. Under fit and forget, nobody needed it. So a DNO often doesn’t know exactly how loaded a particular street transformer is. It estimates.

      That’s changing. Smart meters report half-hourly readings, and over the next couple of years nearly every meter in the country is moving to half-hourly settlement. Matched to the right cables and transformers, that data can show what’s actually happening on each street. Doing that matching well is a data problem. Meters get linked to the wrong substation, readings go missing, and clock changes shuffle the timestamps. It’s very similar to the data-quality problems in any reporting job.

      From DNO to DSO: getting smarter

      So what do you do when a substation is full? There are two broad options.

      Option 1: reinforce. Build more: bigger transformers, new cables, new substations. It works, but it’s slow, often years. It’s expensive. And the cost lands on everyone’s bills. It also means building for a peak that might only happen a few hours a year.

      Option 2: get smarter with what’s already there. This is where the network operator stops being passive and starts actively running the network. A DNO that does this is called a distribution system operator (DSO). In practice it uses three main tools:

      • Flexible connections and ANM. A new generator can connect before the reinforcement is built, if it agrees to be turned down when the network is full. Active Network Management (ANM) watches the constrained points live. When a limit gets close, it automatically tells generators to reduce output. It usually does this in last-in, first-out (LIFO) order, so the most recent connection gets turned down first.
      • Flexibility markets. The DSO pays businesses and households to shift when they use power, or to export from batteries, at the times the network is tight. Paying for a few hours of flexibility can be much cheaper than building a new substation.
      • Smart charging and smart tariffs. Nudging EVs and heat pumps away from the peak, like in the worked example above.
      Where does this come from? Where was the first ANM?

      One of the world’s first ANM schemes went live on Orkney in 2009. The islands had more wind than their cable to the mainland could carry.

      Instead of waiting for a new cable, the operator connected extra wind turbines on the condition that they’d be turned down automatically when the cable was full. Orkney went on to generate more electricity than it used across a year.

      The catch, and where data comes in

      A flexible connection is a gamble for the generator. If you connect early but get turned down 30% of the time, you lose 30% of your income. So before signing, a developer wants to know how often, and how much, they’ll be curtailed.

      That’s a forecasting problem. It needs years of half-hourly data on demand, generation, weather, network limits, outages and the LIFO order. DSOs such as NGED’s publish curtailment data and analysis for exactly this reason. It lets customers judge for themselves whether connecting early is worth it.

      Summary

      • Distribution carries power from the grid supply point to homes and businesses, stepping down 132 kV → 33 kV → 11 kV → 400 V.
      • 11, 33 and 132 are all multiples of 11, most likely from sending power out about 10% above round design voltages.
      • 400 V between phases, 230 V to each house: 400 ÷ √3 ≈ 230.
      • Lower voltage means higher current, so local networks lose more (about 5%) than transmission (about 2%).
      • Six companies run 14 areas. NGED covers South Wales, the South West and the Midlands. Each is a monopoly regulated by Ofgem.
      • The network was built for one-way flow and designed for the winter peak under “fit and forget”.
      • Embedded generation causes reverse power flow, which raises voltage along a cable and can push it past the 253 V limit, and eventually trip solar inverters at about 262 V.
      • A network gets full in two ways: heat (thermal limits) and voltage.
      • Diversity lets a network built for about 2 kW per home work. EVs and heat pumps break it by running for hours at the same times.
      • Most street transformers aren’t monitored. Smart meter data is starting to fill the gap.
      • A DSO actively runs the network, using flexible connections with ANM, flexibility markets and smart charging instead of only building more.
      Key idea

      The distribution network was built for power flowing one way and was left alone. Now power flows both ways, and demand is less spread out. The choice is to build more, slowly and expensively, or to run the network actively, which takes good data and good forecasts.

      Check yourself

      1.A primary substation steps 33 kV down to 11 kV. What’s the turns ratio of its transformer?show answer

      33 ÷ 11 = 3, so the primary coil has three times as many turns as the secondary.

      2.Why do houses get 230 V when the street cable is 400 V?show answer

      The street is three-phase. 400 V is measured between two phase wires. Each house connects to one phase and neutral, which is smaller by √3: 400 ÷ 1.73 ≈ 230 V.

      3.A street cable has 0.2 Ω resistance to the last house. The transformer is set to 240 V. On a sunny afternoon, solar panels push 70 A back to the substation. Is the last house within the legal limit?show answer

      The voltage rises by 70 × 0.2 = 14 V, to 254 V. That’s just above the 253 V legal limit, so the DNO has a problem. The inverters won’t trip yet, though: that happens at about 262 V.

      4.Why can 100 homes share a transformer built for about 2 kW each, when a single shower draws 9 kW?show answer

      Diversity. Not everyone draws their peak at the same moment, so across many homes the combined peak is much lower than the sum of each home’s peak.

      5.A wind farm is offered a flexible connection under LIFO. Why might it want a curtailment forecast before accepting?show answer

      Being curtailed means lost income. It needs to know how often and how much it will be turned down, based on its place in the queue, to judge whether connecting early is worth it compared with waiting for reinforcement.
