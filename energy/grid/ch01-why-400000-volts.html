    Why Does the Grid Run at 400,000 Volts?

    230 volts at your socket, 400,000 on the pylons, 20,000 at the power station. Why all the different numbers? A 150-year-old story about heat, a famous industry feud, and one very simple equation.

    So why does the grid run at 400,000 volts?

    You plug your laptop into a socket at 230 volts. But the cables strung between the pylons you drive past carry up to 400,000 volts. And the generator in the power station makes something else again, around 20,000.

    So why all these different numbers? Why not just make 230 V at the power station and send it straight to your house? It turns out the answer is a 150-year-old story about heat, a famous industry feud, and one very simple equation.

    Contents

      A quick recap: what voltage means

      Voltage is energy per unit of charge (Physics Ch. 1, §1.5). One volt means every coulomb of charge carries one joule of energy.

      So at 230 V, every coulomb flowing through your laptop charger hands over 230 joules. On a 400,000 V line, every coulomb that drops through the full voltage carries 400,000 joules. Same idea, just a much higher “hill” for the charge to roll down.

      How it all started: Faraday’s magnet and coil

      Basically, back in 1831, Michael Faraday noticed something strange. When he moved a magnet in and out of a coil of wire, a current flowed in the wire. Hold the magnet still and the current stopped. It was the change in the magnetic field that made the electricity. This is electromagnetic induction, and every spinning generator today still works this way.

      Where does this come from? Why does only a changing field work?

      Faraday found that the voltage induced in a coil depends on how fast the magnetic field through it changes. A steady magnet gives zero change, so zero voltage.

      That’s why generators spin: spinning a magnet inside coils keeps the field changing, over and over.

      Edison’s problem: the one-mile limit

      For fifty years induction was mostly a lab curiosity. Then, in 1882, Thomas Edison opened some of the first public power stations, one at Holborn Viaduct in London and one on Pearl Street in New York. They made direct current (DC) at about 110 volts.

      There was a catch. At such a low voltage, so much energy was lost as heat in the wires that a power station could only supply homes within about a mile. To light a whole city, you’d need a power station in every neighbourhood.

      The transformer, and the War of the Currents

      The fix came from Faraday’s discovery again: the transformer. Wrap two coils around the same iron core. A changing current in the first coil makes a changing magnetic field, which the iron channels through the second coil and induces a voltage there. Give the second coil more turns and you get a higher voltage. Give it fewer and you get a lower one.

      \dfrac{V_2}{V_1} = \dfrac{N_2}{N_1}
      Where does this come from? Why does the turns ratio set the voltage?

      The iron core makes sure the same changing field passes through every turn of both coils, so each turn picks up the same voltage. A coil with 20 times as many turns picks up 20 times the voltage.

      If the first coil has 100 turns and the second has 2,000, the voltage goes up 20 times, for example from 20,000 V to 400,000 V. And because energy is conserved, the current goes down 20 times: the power in equals the power out (minus a little heat).

      The key word is changing. A transformer only works if the current keeps changing, and DC doesn’t change. So engineers like Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse backed alternating current (AC), which reverses direction many times a second. That way voltage could be stepped up for the journey and back down for homes. Edison fought hard for DC, in what became known as the War of the Currents. AC won.

      Careful: DC has made a comeback

      AC won because transformers were the only practical way to change voltage. Today, power electronics can convert DC voltages too, so very long cables, especially undersea ones, often use high-voltage DC (HVDC), which avoids some of AC’s losses over long distances. Britain’s links to Norway and France are HVDC cables. The grid itself is still AC.

      Building Britain’s National Grid

      In Britain, Sebastian de Ferranti built Deptford Power Station in 1889. It sent AC into central London at 10,000 volts, which was astonishingly high for the time. It showed you could build one big power station far away and send the power in.

      By the 1920s Britain was a mess. Hundreds of local electricity companies ran at different voltages and frequencies, so you couldn’t share power between towns. Parliament stepped in with the Electricity (Supply) Act 1926. A new Central Electricity Board linked everything into one network of 132,000-volt lines, built between 1927 and 1933, and gradually standardised the country on 50 Hz. They called it the National Grid, which is where the name comes from.

      As power stations grew bigger, the voltages grew too:

      1. 1882Edison’s first power stations, DC 110 V
      2. 1889Ferranti’s Deptford sends AC into London 10 kV
      3. 1927–33The National Grid links Britain together 132 kV
      4. 1950sThe “Supergrid” 275 kV
      5. 1960sToday’s backbone 400 kV

      Why higher voltage loses less

      Here’s the physics that drove the whole story. But first, a quick one: what actually is power?

      Energy is how much; power is how fast. Power is the rate energy gets delivered, in joules per second. One joule per second is called a watt (W). A 60 W bulb turns 60 joules into light and heat every second; a 3,000 W kettle gets through 3,000 joules every second. And a power station sending out 100 MW is delivering 100 million joules every second.

      Now the two simple rules. First, the power you’re sending is voltage times current:

      P = V\,I
      Where does this come from? Why is power voltage × current?

      It falls straight out of the definitions. Voltage is joules per coulomb (the energy each bit of charge carries). Current is coulombs per second (how much charge flows past each second).

      Multiply them: (joules per coulomb) × (coulombs per second) = joules per second = watts. The coulombs cancel. A kettle on 230 V drawing 13 A: 230 × 13 ≈ 3,000 W.

      Your electricity bill charges for energy, in kilowatt-hours: 1,000 W running for one hour, which is 3.6 million joules. Power × time = energy.

      Second, the power wasted as heat in the wire depends on the current squared:

      P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R
      Where does this come from? Why current squared?

      From Physics Ch. 1. The voltage lost along a wire is \(V = IR\) (Ohm’s law). The power lost is that voltage drop times the current: \((IR) \times I = I^2 R\).

      Current appears twice, so the loss grows with its square.

      So if you want to send a fixed amount of power, you have a choice. You can use low voltage and high current, or high voltage and low current. Because the loss depends on current squared, high voltage wins by a huge margin.

      Worked example

      You want to send 100 MW (100 million watts) down a line with a resistance of 5 Ω.

      At 20,000 V: current = 100,000,000 ÷ 20,000 = 5,000 A. Loss = 5,000² × 5 = 125 MW. That’s more than you’re sending, so it’s impossible. The wire would melt long before that.

      At 400,000 V: current = 100,000,000 ÷ 400,000 = 250 A. Loss = 250² × 5 = 312,500 W, about 0.3 MW. That’s 0.3% of the power.

      The voltage is 20 times higher, so the current is 20 times lower and the loss is 20² = 400 times smaller.

      That’s the whole reason for pylons and transformers. Across Britain’s high-voltage transmission network, only about 2% of the power is lost.

      Where does this come from? Only 2%, all the way to my house?

      Not quite. The 2% is just the high-voltage transmission network, the pylons. The lower-voltage local networks that carry power the last few miles to homes lose more, roughly another 5%.

      So altogether, somewhere around 7–8% of the electricity generated in Britain is lost on the way. That’s much higher in the final stretch, exactly because the voltage there is lower.

      So why not a million volts?

      If higher voltage is so good, why stop at 400,000? Because very high voltage brings its own problems:

      • The air starts to break down. The electric field right at the surface of the wire gets so strong that it rips electrons off nearby air molecules. This is called corona discharge. You can sometimes hear it as a crackle or buzz under power lines, and it wastes energy. It’s why pylons often carry two or four wires bundled together for each circuit: spreading the charge out weakens the field at each wire’s surface.
      • Everything has to be bigger. Higher voltage needs longer insulators, bigger gaps between wires, taller pylons and wider safety zones. All of that costs money and is harder to get through planning.

      So every country picks a sweet spot. For Britain’s fairly short distances, 400 kV is it. Countries that send power thousands of kilometres go higher: China runs some lines at over 1,000,000 volts.

      The two ends: 20,000 V and 230 V

      Where the 20,000 V at the generator comes from

      The generator is stuck in the middle of two opposite problems:

      • Too high a voltage, and the coils inside fail. The generator’s copper coils are packed tightly into a spinning machine. Higher voltage needs thicker insulation between the wires, which takes up space the copper needs, and raises the risk of sparks jumping across.
      • Too low a voltage, and the current gets huge. Remember \(P = VI\). For a given power, lower voltage means more current, which means thicker conductors and more heat to remove.
      Where does this come from? How big is the current at 20 kV?

      Big power stations use three-phase power: three wires, each carrying its own alternating current, a third of a cycle apart. For three-phase, current per wire = \(P \div (\sqrt{3} \times V)\).

      So a 600 MW generator at 20,000 V pushes about 600,000,000 ÷ (1.73 × 20,000) ≈ 17,000 amps down each conductor. A kettle draws 13 A, so that’s the current of about 1,300 kettles in each one.

      So generators settle at roughly 11,000 to 25,000 volts, often around 20,000. Then a transformer right next to the generator steps the voltage straight up for the journey.

      Where the 230 V at home comes from

      Honestly, this one is partly convention. Britain used 240 V for decades, and much of Europe used 220 V. In the late 1980s Europe agreed on a common figure of 230 V, and Britain officially switched on 1 January 1995, with a tolerance wide enough (216–253 V) that nobody had to change any equipment. So British sockets often still measure close to 240 V.

      Careful: AC voltage isn’t one fixed number

      With AC, the voltage swings up and down 50 times a second. The “230 V” is a kind of average called the RMS value: the steady DC voltage that would deliver the same power. The actual peak is about 325 V.

      V_{\text{RMS}} = \dfrac{V_{\text{peak}}}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{325}{1.414} \approx 230\ \text{V}
      Where does this come from? Why divide by √2?

      Power depends on voltage squared, so it doesn’t care whether the voltage is positive or negative. Over a full swing, a sine wave squared averages out to exactly half its peak value.

      The DC voltage that gives that same average power is the peak ÷ √2. “RMS” just names the recipe: the root of the mean of the square.

      Summary

      • Voltage is energy per coulomb. At 400 kV, every coulomb carries 400,000 joules.
      • Faraday (1831): a changing magnetic field induces a voltage. Generators and transformers both rely on this.
      • Edison’s low-voltage DC (1882) lost too much heat to travel more than about a mile.
      • Transformers can step voltage up and down, but only with AC. So AC won the War of the Currents.
      • Britain linked hundreds of local systems into one 132 kV National Grid (1927–1933), standardised on 50 Hz. It later moved to 275 kV and then 400 kV.
      • Loss = I² × R. Raising the voltage 20 times cuts the current 20 times and the loss 400 times.
      • 400 kV is the sweet spot between lower losses and the cost of corona discharge, bigger pylons and insulators.
      • Generators make about 20 kV, which balances insulation limits against huge currents.
      • 230 V at home is a compromise between Britain’s 240 V and Europe’s 220 V, made official in Britain in 1995.
      Key idea

      The whole shape of the grid comes from one equation. Heat loss grows with current squared, so you send power at high voltage and low current, then use transformers to bring the voltage back down near homes.

      Check yourself

      1.Why can’t a transformer step up DC?show answer

      A transformer relies on induction, which needs a changing magnetic field. DC is a steady current, so it makes a steady field, and a steady field induces no voltage in the second coil.

      2.A transformer’s primary coil has 200 turns and is fed 230 V. The secondary has 10 turns. What voltage comes out?show answer

      \(230 \times 10 \div 200 = 11.5\) V. Twenty times fewer turns, twenty times less voltage.

      3.You send 50 MW at 100,000 V down a line with 4 Ω resistance. How much is lost as heat? What if you doubled the voltage?show answer

      Current = 50,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = 500 A. Loss = 500² × 4 = 1,000,000 W = 1 MW (2%). Double the voltage and the current halves to 250 A, so the loss drops to a quarter: 0.25 MW.

      4.Why doesn’t Britain run its grid at 2,000,000 volts?show answer

      The air around the wires would start breaking down (corona discharge, which wastes energy), and everything would need to be much bigger: longer insulators, taller pylons, wider gaps. For Britain’s short distances, the extra cost isn’t worth the small saving in losses.

      5.Mains is 230 V RMS. What’s the peak voltage?show answer

      \(230 \times \sqrt{2} \approx 325\) V.
