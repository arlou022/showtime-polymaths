China’s Recent AI Market Crash: What Does It Actually Mean?

2 Nov 2026by Arlou·6 min read

In just four months, China’s main index of AI and chip companies (the STAR50) lost about 40% of its value. That’s roughly $480 billion wiped off (Reuters). The strange part is that most of these companies are still doing fine, and their profits kept growing. The companies didn’t fall apart. What fell apart was how much people were willing to pay for a piece of them.

What sets a share’s price

A share is a slice of ownership in a company, which makes it a claim on a slice of the company’s future profits. Its price is simply whatever the last buyer and seller agreed on.

Each buyer works out the most they’d pay from two things:

  • How much profit they expect the company to make in future.
  • The return they demand. This is what a safe option like a bank account pays, plus extra for taking a risk. Say your bank pays 2% interest. Put £1 in and you’re practically guaranteed £1.02 in a year, with almost no risk. Put that same £1 into a company instead, and the company might collapse and take your money with it. So you’d only take that risk if you expect to get back noticeably more than £1.02.

In short, a share’s price is a bet on the company’s future. The more growth people expect, the more they’ll pay today.

How China’s AI stocks got so expensive

In short, people got far too optimistic about how much these companies would grow.

Here’s what that looks like in numbers. Imagine a company making £1 million profit a year, split into 1 million shares, so each share’s slice of the profit is £1 a year. Normally, a share like that might sell for about £15–20. At the peak this summer, people were paying the equivalent of about £250 for it (Reuters). Investors call this the price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E: the price divided by one year’s profit. Here it’s 250, when 15–20 is more normal. It’s the number you’ll see quoted in financial news.

Where does this come from? What’s a “normal” P/E?

P/E = share price ÷ profit per share. A P/E of 20 means you’re paying 20 years of today’s profit up front.

For comparison, Reuters puts the Nasdaq 100, home of America’s big tech companies, at about 30. China’s STAR50 hit nearly 250 at its peak and was still around 100 after the crash.

Paying £250 for £1 of profit a year only makes sense if that £1 grows to something like £25 within about ten years, and almost no company manages that. Many buyers weren’t doing that maths anyway. They bought because prices kept rising, and every rise pulled in more buyers.

Where does this come from? Why “about £25 in ten years”?

Say that in ten years you’d expect the share to be priced “normally” again, at about 18× profit. You’d also want your £250 to have grown, say by around 6% a year, to about £450.

For £450 to be a normal price, the profit per share has to be about £450 ÷ 18 ≈ £25. That’s 25 times today’s £1, which means profits growing by roughly 38% every single year for a decade.

Why it fell

There wasn’t one single cause. A few worries landed at once: prices had got extreme, investors faced tax bills, and the US and other countries were threatening to ban Chinese equipment from their data centres (Reuters). Once prices started to drop, the rush reversed. People sold to avoid losing more, which pushed prices down further.

What didn’t happen is just as important. The companies didn’t start failing, and their profits mostly kept growing (DeepTech Asia). What fell was optimism. Investors went from paying around 250 times a year’s profit to around 100. (That’s a bigger drop than the 40% fall in prices because profits kept rising in the meantime, so each share now has more profit behind it.)

AI companies make up about 42% of the value of China’s whole stock market (Goldman Sachs, via Yahoo Finance). So when they fell, they dragged Chinese shares in general down with them.

So what does this mean for you?

Companies sell shares to the public for one main reason: to raise money. Selling part of the business brings in cash to spend on growth, like factories, chips, data centres and staff. It also lets founders and early investors turn some of their shares into cash.

The catch is that this is mostly a one-off. Say a company lists by selling 1 million new shares at £10 each. Those first buyers pay £10 million, and that money goes to the company. After that, if you buy one of those shares for £250, your money goes to whoever sold it to you, not to the company. Most stock market trading is just investors swapping shares with each other.

So a crash doesn’t empty the company’s bank account. It hurts the next time the company needs money. Suppose it wants another £100 million to build more chips. It raises that by selling new shares, and the share price decides how many it has to sell:

Share priceNew shares needed to raise £100m
£250 (peak)400,000
£100 (after the crash)1,000,000

After the crash, the company has to give away two and a half times as much of itself for the same money. Often it decides not to, and shrinks or shelves its plans instead. Lenders get warier, and new companies hoping to list hold off. That’s what investors now expect in China: one fund manager told Reuters the crash will “put a stop” to many companies’ plans to list.

That’s how a stock crash reaches people who’ve never bought a share:

  1. Share prices fall, so raising money gets expensive.
  2. Companies spend less on new projects, suppliers and hiring.
  3. Their suppliers get fewer orders, so they hire less too.
  4. The economy slows, which can reach jobs well outside tech.

If you have a workplace pension, there may be a direct hit too. Most people saving into a workplace pension today, especially in the private sector, are in a “defined contribution” scheme. Your money is invested, much of it in shares, so falling share prices show up in your balance. “Defined benefit” pensions, which most public-sector workers have, are different. They promise an income based on your salary and years of service, so market falls don’t change what you’ll get.

The big lesson from China is that a technology can be real and important while its shares are still overpriced. AI isn’t going away. The question is whether the price people paid for it was sensible.
