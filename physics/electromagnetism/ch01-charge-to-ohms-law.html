    From Charge to Ohm’s Law

    So what actually is electricity? We build the answer from scratch, one layer at a time: atoms, charge, energy and voltage, then what happens when charge starts to flow.

    So what actually is electricity?

    You hear about it all the time. Your phone’s on charge. You flick a switch and the light comes on. Data centres are now using so much of it that whole power grids are scrambling to keep up. It’s everywhere.

    But if someone stopped you and asked what electricity actually is (what’s physically moving inside the wire, and why it can light a bulb), could you explain it? Most people can’t, and that’s completely normal. We use the words “volts” and “amps” without ever being shown what’s underneath them.

    So that’s what this chapter does. We’ll build the whole picture from scratch, one layer at a time, starting with the smallest pieces of stuff there are. By the end, voltage, current and resistance will each mean something concrete you can picture. Every number and formula gets explained too: look for the yellow “Where does this come from?” notes.

    By the end of this chapter you’ll be able to
    • say what charge is, and why most things are electrically neutral;
    • explain voltage as energy per unit of charge, with real numbers;
    • explain why copper conducts and plastic doesn’t;
    • use \(I = Q/t\) and \(V = IR\) to work out what a simple circuit does.

    Contents

      Atoms: what everything is made of

      Let’s start at the very bottom. Everything around you (your desk, the air, your phone, you) is made of atoms. And atoms are made of three kinds of even smaller particle:

      • Protons sit in the middle of the atom, in a tiny dense core called the nucleus. They have a positive charge.
      • Neutrons sit in the nucleus too. They have no charge at all.
      • Electrons buzz around the outside. They have a negative charge, and they’re tiny: about 1,836 times lighter than a proton.
      Where does this come from? How do we know electrons are 1,836× lighter?

      Nobody can put an electron on a scale. Instead, physicists send particles through a magnetic field at the same speed and watch how much their paths curve. An electron and a proton carry the same amount of charge, so the field pushes them equally hard, and the lighter one bends far more.

      Comparing the two bends gives the mass ratio: about 1,836. Today the masses are known very precisely: \(9.109 \times 10^{-31}\) kg for the electron and \(1.673 \times 10^{-27}\) kg for the proton.

      Here’s the neat part. A normal atom has exactly as many electrons as protons, so the positives and negatives cancel out and the atom is neutral overall. That’s why your desk doesn’t zap you. But if an atom loses or gains an electron, the balance tips and it ends up with a net charge. We call that an ion.

      One more thing to hold on to: protons are locked inside the nucleus and basically never go anywhere. In everyday electricity, it’s always the electrons that move. That one idea is the whole story of this chapter.

      Careful: this picture isn’t to scale

      If the nucleus were the size of a pea, the whole atom would be about the size of a football stadium. Atoms are almost entirely empty space. And electrons don’t really travel in neat circles: quantum mechanics swaps “orbits” for fuzzy clouds of probability. The simple picture works perfectly well for everything in this chapter, though.

      Charge: the push and the pull

      So what actually is this “charge” the protons and electrons are carrying? To answer that, it helps to go back to how people first noticed it.

      Basically, back in the day (we’re talking ancient Greece), people spotted something odd. Rub a piece of amber with fur, and suddenly it starts pulling bits of straw and fluff towards it. The Greek word for amber was ēlektron, which is where our word “electricity” comes from.

      Fast forward to the 1700s, and experimenters found it goes both ways. Some rubbed objects attract each other, and others push each other away. The French scientist Charles du Fay worked out that there must be two kinds of whatever was causing this. Benjamin Franklin later gave the two kinds the names we still use: positive and negative. And the property itself got a name too: charge.

      Here’s the honest bit: that’s still pretty much where physics is today. Charge isn’t made of anything simpler. We know it by what it does:

      • Like charges repel: electron pushes electron, proton pushes proton.
      • Opposite charges attract: an electron and a proton pull towards each other.
      • The force gets weaker with distance. Double the distance and the force drops to a quarter.

      That last rule has a name, the inverse-square law, and when you write the whole thing out properly you get Coulomb’s law:

      F = \dfrac{k\, q_1 q_2}{r^2}
      Where does this come from? Who came up with this, and why this shape?

      In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured it directly. He hung a thin rod from a very fine wire and measured how far charged balls pushed it round at different distances. Halve the distance, and the twist was four times stronger.

      Why \(r^2\)? It’s geometry. A charge’s influence spreads out in every direction, like light from a bulb. At distance \(r\) it’s spread over a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\). Double the distance and the same influence covers four times the area, so it’s a quarter as strong. Light and gravity fade the same way for the same reason.

      Why \(q_1 \times q_2\)? Double either charge and the force doubles, so the force is proportional to both.

      And \(k\)? It just converts “charge × charge ÷ distance²” into newtons. Its value, \(9 \times 10^9\), is so big because the coulomb is a huge amount of charge.

      Read it in words: the bigger the two charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), the bigger the force. And the force shrinks with the square of the distance \(r\) between them. You don’t need to memorise anything else. The shape is what matters: 2× the distance gives ¼ of the force, 3× the distance gives \(1/9\).

      How much charge?

      In 1897 J.J. Thomson discovered the electron, and it turned out every single electron carries exactly the same amount of charge. It’s the smallest lump of charge you ever find on its own, and it’s called the elementary charge:

      e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}
      Where does this come from? How did anyone measure something that tiny?

      In 1909 Robert Millikan sprayed tiny oil droplets between two metal plates and switched on a voltage. By adjusting it until a droplet hovered (the electric push exactly balancing gravity), he could work out each droplet’s charge.

      The charges were never random. They were always 1, 2, 3… times the same basic lump: about \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C. That lump is the electron’s charge.

      Since 2019 it isn’t even measured any more: the value \(1.602176634 \times 10^{-19}\) C is fixed exactly, and other units are defined from it.

      The C stands for coulomb, the unit we measure charge in. It’s a convenient, human-sized unit: 1 coulomb is about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons’ worth of charge, which is 6.24 billion billion. A proton carries exactly +\(e\) and an electron exactly −\(e\), which is why equal numbers of them cancel perfectly.

      Where does this come from? Why exactly 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons?

      Honestly, there’s nothing special about that number. The coulomb was chosen as a handy unit (via the amp: 1 coulomb is what flows when 1 amp runs for 1 second) long before anyone knew electrons existed.

      So when the electron’s charge was finally measured, the number of electrons in one coulomb just came out as whatever it came out as: \(1 \div (1.602 \times 10^{-19}) \approx 6.24 \times 10^{18}\). It’s like asking why a foot isn’t a round number of atoms long.

      Key idea

      Charge comes in fixed lumps of \(e\). Like charges repel, opposite charges attract, and the force falls off as \(1/r^2\).

      Energy: the capacity to make things move

      OK, so charges push and pull on each other. Before electricity can do anything useful, like lighting a bulb or spinning a motor, we need one more idea: energy.

      Here’s the everyday version. Energy is what it takes to make something move against a force. Push a heavy box across the floor and you’re fighting friction the whole way; that effort is energy you’re handing over. The precise version is that the energy you transfer, called work, is force times distance:

      W = F\,d
      Where does this come from? Why force × distance?

      Common sense first: pushing twice as hard takes twice the effort, and so does pushing twice as far. So effort grows with both, which means multiplying them.

      The deeper reason physicists picked exactly this quantity is that it’s the one that balances the books. Whatever force × distance you put in comes back out, exactly, as motion, height, heat or stored energy.

      The unit is named after James Joule. In the 1840s he showed that a falling weight turning a paddle-wheel in water warms the water by the same amount every time. Work and heat are the same stuff.

      Push something with 1 newton for 1 metre, and you’ve transferred 1 joule of energy. (A newton is about the weight of a small apple, so lifting an apple one metre takes roughly one joule.)

      Where does this come from? Why is a newton “about an apple”?

      Weight is mass × the pull of gravity, which on Earth is about 9.8 newtons for every kilogram. A small apple is about 100 g, so its weight is \(0.1 \times 9.8 \approx 1\) N. (A nice coincidence, given the newton is named after Isaac Newton.)

      Energy can also be stored and let out later: in a stretched spring, a weight lifted up high, or (as we’re about to see) charges pulled apart. And here’s the really important bit: energy is never created or destroyed. It only gets moved around and changed from one form to another. Physics actually defines energy by what it does, a bit like charge. Richard Feynman famously said we don’t really know what energy is, only that when you add it all up, the total always stays the same.

      Worked example

      You push a box 3 m across the floor with a steady 20 N. Work done \(= 20 \times 3 = 60\) J. So where did those 60 J go? On a rough floor, almost all of it became heat in the box and the floor. None of it vanished.

      Putting them together: an electron and a proton

      Now let’s combine the two ideas, because this is where electricity really starts.

      Picture an electron sitting right next to a proton. They attract, so if you want to pull the electron away, you have to fight that pull the whole way. Force × distance = work done = energy stored in the separation. Let go, and the electron rushes straight back, releasing all that energy.

      If that sounds familiar, it should. It’s exactly like lifting a weight: lifting it stores energy against gravity, and dropping it releases that energy. Pulling charges apart is electricity’s version of lifting a weight.

      Try pulling it further and further in the figure, and you’ll notice something: each extra step stores a little less energy than the one before. That’s because the attraction gets weaker with distance (), so each extra centimetre is easier to pull.

      Voltage: energy per unit of charge

      Now we can finally answer what a battery actually does. A battery is basically a charge-separating machine. It uses chemistry to do the pulling, keeping a crowd of electrons piled up at one end and a shortage at the other. Connect a wire, and those electrons can finally rush round and release the stored energy.

      Voltage is how we measure that stored energy, divided per unit of charge:

      V = \dfrac{W}{Q}
      Where does this come from? Why 9 volts, and where does “volt” come from?

      The unit is named after Alessandro Volta, who built the first battery in 1800: a stack of zinc and copper discs separated by cloth soaked in salt water.

      A battery’s voltage is set by its chemistry, by which metals and chemicals are reacting. A single standard cell gives about 1.5 V. A square 9 V battery is literally six 1.5 V cells stacked inside one case: \(6 \times 1.5 = 9\).

      Its unit is the volt, and one volt is simply one joule per coulomb: 1 V = 1 J/C. Let’s make that concrete with a 9 V battery:

      • Between your 9 V battery’s terminals, every coulomb gives up 9 joules as it flows round the circuit.
      • One coulomb is 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons, so each single electron gives up 9 ÷ (6.24 × 10¹⁸) ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ joules.

      Why bother dividing by charge? Because it makes voltage a property of the battery, not of how much charge happens to flow. Whether 1 electron goes round or a trillion, each one gets the same 9 V’s worth of energy per unit of charge.

      Analogy: voltage is like height

      Think of a 9 V battery as lifting each coulomb up a “9-joule hill”. Height tells you how much energy each kilogram of water gains by being up there; voltage tells you how much each coulomb gains. More water rolling down means more energy in total, but the energy per kilogram depends only on the height.

      Flip round and you get a handy form: \(W = VQ\). Total energy = voltage × charge. So five coulombs through a 9 V battery deliver 45 J.

      Conductors and insulators: why copper but not plastic?

      So a battery pushes electrons round a wire. But hang on: why can electrons move through a copper wire, but not through the plastic coating around it? It all comes down to how tightly each atom holds on to its outermost electrons.

      In a metal like copper, each atom has one outer electron that it only holds on to loosely. When billions of copper atoms pack together into a solid, those outer electrons stop belonging to any particular atom. They roam through the whole metal as a “sea” of free electrons, drifting around a fixed grid of positive ions (the atoms minus their outer electron). Because they’re already free to wander, the slightest push from a battery gets them all drifting the same way. A material with free charges like this is called a conductor.

      In plastic, it’s a completely different story. The electrons are locked into the chemical bonds holding the atoms together, and each one is held tight. Apply a voltage and they lean a little towards the positive side, but they can’t actually leave. No free charges means no current: plastic is an insulator.

      And that’s exactly why electrical wires are copper wrapped in plastic. The copper carries the current, and the plastic stops it going anywhere it shouldn’t, like into your hand.

      Careful: “insulator” means very hard, not impossible

      Push hard enough, with a big enough voltage, and you can rip electrons free even from an insulator. Lightning is exactly that, happening in air. And somewhere in between conductors and insulators sit the semiconductors, like silicon, whose ability to conduct can be switched on and off. Every computer chip is built from them.

      Current: charge on the move

      Once charge is flowing, the natural question is: how much is flowing? Imagine standing at one spot on the wire and counting the charge that goes past you each second. That rate is the current:

      I = \dfrac{Q}{t}

      Current is measured in amperes (amps, A), named after the French physicist André-Marie Ampère. One amp is one coulomb per second, which is about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons passing any point in the wire every single second. For a feel of the numbers: a phone charger supplies 1–2 A, and a UK kettle draws about 13 A.

      Which way does current flow?

      Remember Benjamin Franklin naming the two kinds of charge? This is where his choice comes back to bite. He picked which kind to call “positive” in the 1700s, well over a century before anyone discovered the electron. By pure bad luck, his choice made the electron the negative one.

      So by convention, current is drawn flowing from + to −. That’s called conventional current. But the electrons actually doing the moving are negative, so they really go the other way, from − to +. Both describe the same flow, and circuit diagrams still use Franklin’s convention.

      Careful: electrons are slow

      In a typical 1 mm² copper wire carrying 1 A, the electrons drift along at less than 0.1 mm per second. That’s slower than a snail. So why does the light come on the instant you flick the switch? Because the wire is already full of electrons. The push travels down the wire at close to the speed of light, like pushing a tube that’s already packed with marbles: shove one in at one end, and one pops out the other end straight away.

      And the energy isn’t carried inside the electrons like parcels on a conveyor belt either. It actually travels through the electric and magnetic fields around the wire. We’ll come back to that when we get to magnetism.

      Where does this come from? How do we know they move under 0.1 mm/s?

      You can work it out. Current = (free electrons per cubic metre) × (wire’s cross-section) × (charge per electron) × (drift speed).

      Copper has about \(8.5 \times 10^{28}\) free electrons per cubic metre (one per atom). The wire is \(10^{-6}\) m², and each electron carries \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C. For 1 A:

      \(v = \dfrac{1}{8.5\times10^{28} \times 10^{-6} \times 1.6\times10^{-19}} \approx 7 \times 10^{-5}\) m/s, about 0.07 mm per second.

      Resistance and Ohm’s law

      Last piece. As electrons drift through copper, they keep crashing into the metal’s ions, which are constantly jiggling about. Every crash hands over a bit of energy and makes the ions jiggle more, and that’s why wires warm up. This opposition to the flow is called resistance, \(R\), and it’s measured in ohms (Ω).

      In the 1820s a German schoolteacher called Georg Ohm tested wires of different lengths, and noticed something simple. For a given wire, double the voltage and you get double the current. That’s Ohm’s law:

      V = I\,R
      Where does this come from? Why would current be proportional to voltage?

      Ohm found it by experiment (published in 1827), but here’s why it works. The voltage creates a push on every free electron. Between crashes, each electron speeds up a little; then it hits an ion and loses that speed again.

      The harder the push, the faster the average drift, and the drift is directly proportional to the push. Double the voltage, double the drift speed, double the current. Resistance just measures how much push it takes to get each amp flowing.

      It’s the same rule written three ways: \(I = V/R\) (more voltage gives more current; more resistance gives less), and \(R = V/I\) (resistance is how many volts it takes to push each amp through).

      Analogy: water in pipes

      Voltage is like water pressure, current is like the flow rate, and resistance is like how narrow the pipe is. More pressure gives more flow; a narrower pipe gives less flow at the same pressure.

      Worked example

      A 9 V battery is connected across a 300 Ω resistor. Current \(I = V/R = 9/300 = 0.03\) A, which is 30 milliamps. Every second 0.03 C flows, and each coulomb gives up 9 J, so 0.27 J of heat appears in the resistor every second. “Joules per second” has its own name, the watt (power), and it comes up properly in the next chapter.

      Careful: Ohm’s law isn’t a law of nature

      It’s a property some materials have, not a rule everything follows. A filament bulb’s resistance rises as it heats up, and a diode barely lets current through in one direction at all. A material that does follow it (an ohmic conductor) gives a straight line through zero on an I–V graph, exactly like .

      Summary

      So, what actually is electricity? It’s charge (almost always electrons) being pushed round a loop by stored energy, and handing that energy over as it goes. Here’s the whole picture, layer by layer:

      • Atoms contain protons (+), neutrons (0) and electrons (−). Equal numbers of protons and electrons make an atom neutral.
      • Charge comes in lumps of \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C. Like charges repel and opposites attract, with a force that falls off as \(1/r^2\).
      • Energy is the capacity to make things move against a force: \(W = Fd\). It’s never created or destroyed.
      • Pulling charges apart stores energy. Voltage is that energy per coulomb: \(V = W/Q\), and 1 V = 1 J/C.
      • Metals have free electrons (conductors); plastics don’t (insulators).
      • Current is charge per second, \(I = Q/t\). Electrons drift slowly from − to +; conventional current is drawn from + to −.
      • Resistance opposes the flow and turns energy into heat. For ohmic conductors, \(V = IR\).

      Check yourself

      1.An oxygen atom has 8 protons. Right now it has 10 electrons. What’s its overall charge?show answer

      8 − 10 = −2, so its charge is −2\(e\) = −3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. It’s gained two extra electrons, which makes it a negative ion.

      2.Two charges 3 cm apart push each other with some force. You move them to 6 cm apart. What happens to the force?show answer

      The distance doubles, so the force drops to (½)² = ¼ of what it was. That’s the inverse-square law.

      3.You push a sofa 4 m with a steady 50 N. How much work do you do?show answer

      \(W = Fd = 50 \times 4 = 200\) J.

      4.A 12 V car battery pushes 3 C of charge round a circuit. How much energy does it deliver?show answer

      \(W = VQ = 12 \times 3 = 36\) J. Each coulomb carries 12 J, and there are three of them.

      5.A current of 2 A flows for 10 seconds. How much charge went past, and roughly how many electrons is that?show answer

      \(Q = It = 2 \times 10 = 20\) C. That’s 20 × 6.24 × 10¹⁸ ≈ 1.25 × 10²⁰ electrons.

      6.A 6 Ω resistor is connected to a 12 V supply. What current flows?show answer

      \(I = V/R = 12/6 = 2\) A.

      7.Why are electrical wires made of copper wrapped in plastic?show answer

      Copper has free electrons, so it conducts. Plastic’s electrons are locked in its bonds, so it insulates. The copper carries the current, and the plastic keeps it safely inside the wire.
