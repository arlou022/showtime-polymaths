    From Charge to Ohm’s Law

    What electricity actually is, built one layer at a time: atoms, charge, energy and voltage, then what happens when charge starts to flow.

    Contents

      Atoms: what everything is made of

      Matter is made of atoms, and atoms are made of three kinds of particle:

      • Proton: sits in the nucleus, the tiny dense centre of the atom. It has a positive charge.
      • Neutron: also in the nucleus. It has no charge.
      • Electron: around the nucleus. It has a negative charge and is about 1,836 times lighter than a proton.

      A normal atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so its charges cancel and it is neutral overall. If it loses or gains an electron, the balance tips and the atom has a net charge. We call it an ion.

      Protons are locked inside the nucleus and almost never move about. In everyday electricity it is always the electrons that move. Hold on to that thought: it is the whole story of this chapter.

      Careful: this picture is not to scale

      If the nucleus were the size of a pea, the whole atom would be roughly the size of a football stadium. Atoms are almost entirely empty space. And electrons don’t really follow neat circular tracks: quantum mechanics replaces “orbits” with fuzzy clouds of probability. The simple picture is perfectly good for everything in this chapter.

      Charge: the push and the pull

      Charge is the property that makes particles push or pull on each other. That is honestly the most physics can say. Charge isn’t made of anything simpler; it is defined by how it behaves:

      • Like charges repel: electron pushes electron, proton pushes proton.
      • Opposite charges attract: an electron and a proton pull together.
      • The force gets weaker with distance. Double the distance and the force drops to a quarter.

      That last rule has a name, the inverse-square law, and the full version is called Coulomb’s law:

      F = \dfrac{k\, q_1 q_2}{r^2}

      Read it in words: the bigger the two charges \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), the bigger the force; and the force shrinks with the square of the distance \(r\). The \(k\) is just a constant that makes the units come out right (about \(9 \times 10^9\)). You don’t need to memorise it. The shape of the rule is what matters: 2× the distance gives ¼ of the force, and 3× the distance gives \(1/9\).

      How much charge?

      Every electron carries exactly the same amount of charge, called the elementary charge:

      e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}

      The C stands for coulomb, the unit of charge. The coulomb is just a convenient human-sized unit: 1 coulomb is about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons’ worth of charge (that is 6.24 billion billion). A proton carries exactly +\(e\) and an electron exactly −\(e\). That is why equal numbers of them cancel perfectly.

      Key idea

      Charge comes in fixed lumps of \(e\). Like charges repel, opposite charges attract, and the force falls off as \(1/r^2\).

      Energy: the capacity to make things move

      Energy is the capacity to make something move against a force. The precise version is that work, the energy you transfer, equals force times distance:

      W = F\,d

      Push something with 1 newton for 1 metre and you have transferred 1 joule of energy. (A newton is roughly the weight of a small apple, so lifting an apple one metre takes about one joule.)

      Energy can be stored and released later: in a stretched spring, a lifted weight, or (as we are about to see) separated charges. And it is never created or destroyed, only moved around and converted from one form to another. As with charge, physics defines energy by what it does. Richard Feynman famously said that we don’t really know what energy is, only that when you add it all up, the total always stays the same.

      Worked example

      You push a box 3 m across the floor with a steady 20 N. Work done \(= 20 \times 3 = 60\) J. Where did the 60 J go? On a rough floor, almost all of it became heat in the box and the floor. None of it vanished.

      Putting them together: an electron and a proton

      Now combine the two ideas. Pull an electron away from a proton. They attract, so you have to pull against that attraction over a distance. Force × distance = work done = energy stored in the separation. Let go, and the electron rushes back, releasing that energy.

      This is exactly like lifting a weight. Lifting stores energy against gravity; dropping it releases that energy. Separated charges are electricity’s version of a lifted weight.

      Notice one thing in the figure: the further you pull, the more energy is stored, but each extra step adds a little less. That is because the attraction gets weaker with distance (), so each extra centimetre takes less effort.

      Voltage: energy per unit of charge

      A battery is a device that keeps charges separated, using chemistry to do the pulling. Voltage measures that stored energy, divided per unit of charge:

      V = \dfrac{W}{Q}

      Its unit is the volt, and one volt is simply one joule per coulomb: 1 V = 1 J/C. Concrete values, using a 9 V battery:

      • Between your 9 V battery’s terminals, each coulomb gives up 9 joules when it flows around the circuit.
      • One coulomb is 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons, so each single electron gives up 9 ÷ (6.24 × 10¹⁸) ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ joules.

      Dividing by charge is useful because it makes voltage a property of the battery, not of how much charge happens to flow. Whether 1 electron flows or a trillion, each one gets the same 9 V’s worth of energy per unit of charge.

      Analogy: voltage is like height

      A 9 V battery lifts each coulomb up a “9-joule hill”. Height tells you how much energy each kilogram of water gains by being up there; voltage tells you how much each coulomb gains. More water rolling down means more energy in total, but the energy per kilogram depends only on the height.

      Rearranging gives the handy form \(W = VQ\): total energy = voltage × charge. Five coulombs through a 9 V battery deliver 45 J.

      Conductors and insulators: why copper but not plastic?

      The natural next layer is what happens inside a wire. Why can electrons move freely through a metal but not through plastic? It comes down to how tightly each atom holds on to its outermost electrons.

      In a metal like copper, each atom has one outer electron that is held only weakly. When billions of copper atoms pack together into a solid, those outer electrons stop belonging to any particular atom. They roam through the whole metal as a “sea” of free electrons, flowing around a fixed grid of positive ions (the atoms minus their outer electron). Because these electrons are already free to wander, the slightest push from a battery sets them all drifting in one direction. A material with free charges like this is a conductor.

      In plastic, the electrons are locked into the chemical bonds that hold the atoms together. Each one is held tightly. Apply a voltage and they lean slightly towards the positive side, but they can’t leave. No free charges means no current: plastic is an insulator.

      That is exactly why electrical wires are copper wrapped in plastic. The copper carries the current, and the plastic stops it going anywhere it shouldn’t, such as into your hand.

      Careful: “insulator” means very hard, not impossible

      A large enough voltage can rip electrons free even from an insulator. Lightning is exactly that, happening in air. And between conductors and insulators sit the semiconductors, such as silicon, whose ability to conduct can be switched on and off. Computer chips are built from them.

      Current: charge on the move

      When charges flow, the natural question is how much flows per second. That rate is the current:

      I = \dfrac{Q}{t}

      Current is measured in amperes (amps, A). One amp is one coulomb per second: about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons passing any point in the wire every second. A phone charger supplies 1–2 A; a UK kettle draws about 13 A.

      Which way does current flow?

      This is a historical accident. In the 1700s Benjamin Franklin chose which kind of charge to call “positive”, more than a century before anyone discovered the electron (in 1897). His choice made the electron negative. So by convention, current is drawn flowing from + to −; this is called conventional current. The electrons themselves, being negative, actually move the other way, from − to +. Both describe the same flow, and circuit diagrams use conventional current.

      Careful: electrons are slow

      In a typical 1 mm² copper wire carrying 1 A, the electrons drift along at less than 0.1 mm per second, slower than a snail. Yet a light comes on the instant you flick the switch. The wire is already full of electrons, so the push travels down it at close to the speed of light, like pushing a tube already packed with marbles: one goes in and one pops out of the far end immediately.

      And the energy isn’t carried inside the electrons like parcels on a conveyor belt. It actually travels through the electric and magnetic fields around the wire. We will come back to that when we reach magnetism.

      Resistance and Ohm’s law

      As electrons drift through copper, they keep bumping into the vibrating ions of the metal. Each bump hands over some energy, making the ions vibrate more, and the wire heats up. This opposition to the flow is called resistance, \(R\), measured in ohms (Ω).

      For many materials, including metals at a steady temperature, the current is proportional to the voltage: double the push and you get double the flow. That is Ohm’s law:

      V = I\,R

      It is the same rule written three ways: \(I = V/R\) (more voltage gives more current; more resistance gives less), and \(R = V/I\) (resistance is how many volts it takes to push each amp through).

      Analogy: water in pipes

      Voltage is like water pressure, current is like flow rate, and resistance is like how narrow the pipe is. More pressure gives more flow; a narrower pipe gives less flow at the same pressure.

      Worked example

      A 9 V battery is connected across a 300 Ω resistor. Current \(I = V/R = 9/300 = 0.03\) A, which is 30 milliamps. Every second, 0.03 C flows, and each coulomb gives up 9 J, so 0.27 J of heat appears in the resistor every second. “Joules per second” has its own name, the watt (power), and it comes up properly in the next chapter.

      Careful: Ohm’s law isn’t universal

      It is a property that some materials have, not a law of nature. A filament bulb’s resistance rises as it heats up, and a diode barely lets current through one way at all. A material that does obey it (an ohmic conductor) gives a straight line through the origin on an I–V graph, exactly what draws.

      Summary

      • Atoms contain protons (+), neutrons (0) and electrons (−). Equal numbers of protons and electrons make an atom neutral.
      • Charge comes in lumps of \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C. Like charges repel and opposites attract, with a force that falls off as \(1/r^2\).
      • Energy is the capacity to make things move against a force: \(W = Fd\). It is never created or destroyed.
      • Separating charges stores energy. Voltage is that energy per coulomb: \(V = W/Q\), and 1 V = 1 J/C.
      • Metals have free electrons (conductors); plastics don’t (insulators).
      • Current is charge per second, \(I = Q/t\). Electrons drift slowly from − to +; conventional current is drawn from + to −.
      • Resistance opposes the flow and turns energy into heat. For ohmic conductors, \(V = IR\).

      Check yourself

      1.An oxygen atom has 8 protons. Right now it has 10 electrons. What is its overall charge?show answer

      8 − 10 = −2, so its charge is −2\(e\) = −3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. It has gained two electrons, which makes it a negative ion.

      2.Two charges 3 cm apart push each other with some force. You move them to 6 cm apart. What happens to the force?show answer

      The distance doubles, so the force drops to (½)² = ¼ of what it was (the inverse-square law).

      3.You push a sofa 4 m with a steady 50 N. How much work do you do?show answer

      \(W = Fd = 50 \times 4 = 200\) J.

      4.A 12 V car battery pushes 3 C of charge around a circuit. How much energy is delivered?show answer

      \(W = VQ = 12 \times 3 = 36\) J. Each coulomb carries 12 J, and there are three of them.

      5.A current of 2 A flows for 10 seconds. How much charge passed, and roughly how many electrons is that?show answer

      \(Q = It = 2 \times 10 = 20\) C. That is 20 × 6.24 × 10¹⁸ ≈ 1.25 × 10²⁰ electrons.

      6.A 6 Ω resistor is connected to a 12 V supply. What current flows?show answer

      \(I = V/R = 12/6 = 2\) A.

      7.Why are electrical wires made of copper wrapped in plastic?show answer

      Copper has free electrons, so it conducts. Plastic’s electrons are locked in bonds, so it insulates. The copper carries the current and the plastic keeps it safely inside the wire.
